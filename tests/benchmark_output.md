## 1. Relative velocity — football

**Concept:** Relative velocity  
**Interest:** football  
**Level:** Class 11

---

1. SCENARIO
Imagine you are a defender running down the wing at 8 m/s, trying to catch an opponent who is running at 10 m/s in the same direction. From your perspective, the opponent isn't pulling away at their full 10 m/s speed; instead, they appear to be moving away from you at a slower 2 m/s. This perceived speed is how fast the opponent is moving relative to you.

    your speed relative to the ground  →  v_B (velocity of observer)
    opponent's speed relative to the ground →  v_A (velocity of object)
    opponent's speed as seen by you   →  v_AB (relative velocity)

The analogy breaks down if you and the opponent are not moving in the same straight line, as real football involves movement in multiple directions.

2. FORMAL RESTATEMENT
**Relative velocity** describes the velocity of an object A as observed from a moving frame of reference B. For motion in one dimension, if v_A is the velocity of object A and v_B is the velocity of object B, both relative to a common stationary frame, then the velocity of A relative to B is given by v_AB = v_A − v_B.

3. SELF-CHECK QUESTION

## 4. Projectile motion — football

**Concept:** Projectile motion  
**Interest:** football  
**Level:** Class 11

---

1. SCENARIO
Imagine you're taking a free kick. When you kick the ball, it launches into the air, following a curved path before landing. The harder you kick it (greater initial velocity) and the higher you aim it (greater launch angle), the farther it will travel and the higher it will go. Once it leaves your foot, the only significant force acting on it is gravity, pulling it downwards, which makes it eventually fall back to the ground.

    kicking the ball        →  initial velocity (magnitude and direction)
    ball's curved path      →  trajectory
    gravity                 →  acceleration due to gravity
    distance ball travels   →  range
    highest point of ball   →  maximum height

The analogy breaks down because in real football, air resistance significantly affects the ball's flight, especially with spin, which is usually ignored in basic projectile motion.

2. FORMAL RESTATEMENT
**Projectile motion** describes the motion of an object thrown or projected into the air, subject only to the acceleration of gravity. Its path, called a **trajectory**, is typically parabolic. The motion can be analyzed by separating it into independent horizontal motion (constant velocity, ignoring air resistance) and vertical motion (constant acceleration due to gravity, g = 9.8 m/s² downwards).

3. SELF-CHECK QUESTION
If you kick a football with the same initial speed but a lower launch angle, how would the maximum height and the time the ball spends in the air change, and why?

## 5. Free fall — gaming

**Concept:** Free fall  
**Interest:** gaming  
**Level:** Class 11

---

1. SCENARIO
Imagine your character in a game jumps off a cliff. If the game has realistic physics, your character will start falling slowly, then pick up speed the longer they are in the air. This is because the game engine constantly applies a downward "gravity force." If you fall long enough and there's no air resistance, you'll keep getting faster and faster until you hit the ground.

    character falling → object in free fall
    game engine's gravity force → acceleration due to gravity (g)
    increasing fall speed → increasing velocity
    time in the air → time (t)

The analogy breaks because most games have a "terminal velocity" where your character stops accelerating downwards, but in true free fall without air resistance, acceleration is constant and velocity increases indefinitely.

2. FORMAL RESTATEMENT
**Free fall** is the motion of an object solely under the influence of **gravity**, with negligible air resistance. Near the Earth's surface, all objects in free fall experience a constant downward **acceleration due to gravity**, denoted as 'g', which is approximately 9.8 m/s². The equations of motion for free fall are derived from constant acceleration kinematics: v = u + gt, h = ut + (1/2)gt², and v² = u² + 2gh, where 'u' is initial velocity, 'v' is final velocity, 't' is time, and 'h' is displacement.

3. SELF-CHECK QUESTION

## 8. Work-energy theorem — football

**Concept:** Work-energy theorem  
**Interest:** football  
**Level:** Class 11

---

1. SCENARIO
Imagine you're a football player running with the ball. When your teammate passes the ball to you, the force of the pass increases the ball's speed. The "work" done by the pass is the force applied by your teammate multiplied by the distance the ball travels while the force is acting (i.e., the moment of contact). This work directly corresponds to how much the ball's **kinetic energy** (energy of motion) changes, making it go faster. If you then kick the ball with a force in the opposite direction, you do negative work, and the ball slows down, reducing its kinetic energy.

    force of the pass/kick → F (net force)
    distance of contact    → d (displacement)
    F × d                  → W (work done on the ball)
    change in ball's speed → ΔKE = (1/2)m(v_f² − v_i²)

The analogy breaks because in real football, the mass of the ball is constant; in physics, the work-energy theorem applies even if mass changes (though this is rare in introductory problems).

2. FORMAL RESTATEMENT
The **work-energy theorem** states that the net work done on an object equals the change in its **kinetic energy**: W_net = ΔKE = (1/2)m·v_f² − (1/2)m·v_i².

3. SELF-CHECK QUESTION
If a player kicks a stationary football with a certain force over a certain distance, and then another player kicks the same ball with twice the force over the same distance, how much greater is the change in the ball's kinetic energy in the second case?
